The Full Story

Hi, my name is Xavier Maquilón, and this is my journey through tech, from the first time I saw a computer to where I am today, September 2026.

One thing worth mentioning is that technology was not always easy to get here in Ecuador. A lot of the "new" technology available here was already three or four years old compared to what was available in the United States. That made things a little different, but it also made every new piece of technology feel special.

This is going to be a long wall of text, so be advised.

Chapter 1 — The First Computer

The first time I saw a computer was when I saw my dad working with one.

I think he was transcribing handwritten documents from manila folders into the PC and then printing them with a dot-matrix printer. I still remember connecting them with a parallel-port cable.

I think it was an IBM PC. There was no graphical interface, just a black screen with what I remember as green text. It had a horizontal PC case with two switches and a floppy disk drive. That was it.

I remember typing my name, my brother's name, and my parents' names into it over and over again. Everything I heard, I tried to type as much of it as I could.

Then my dad printed about five pages of it.

It was the best thing ever.

I loved those cables, by the way. Even today, I'm still amazed by VGA cables. You could practically lift the monitor by the cable, or the motherboard and the PC case.

I didn't know it at the time, but that little computer had already planted something in my head.

Chapter 2 — The Home PC

After that, I remember my mom buying a "home PC" for all of us to use. I think it was a Compaq.

This time, it was the real deal: a vertical PC case, a floppy drive, a CD-ROM drive, and, of course, the monitor screen protector with those little hangers that you had to put into the holes on the monitor.

This was when I really started playing around with computers.

It ran Windows 98 and came with a bunch of pre-installed games. Most of them were demos, except for 3D Pinball: Space Cadet.

I played that one a lot.

There was also Doom, Wolfenstein 3D, and Duke Nukem 3D. The first three were demos, so I could only play the first level, but point-and-click games have always had a special place in my heart. Day of the Tentacle was one of those games I remember being there.

I also had a blast drawing things in Paint. I think I enjoyed that even more than the demos.

I remember drawing four default-sized squares with whatever I could think of inside them. Then I would use a program for printing photos, I think it was Lexmark software, put the four drawings into it, and print them.

It came out looking like a comic strip.

It was amazing.

Windows itself was also incredibly approachable. As a kid, I could just mess around with it and discover things. I changed themes, played with screensavers, and my favorite one was "Mystery."

Back then, computers didn't feel complicated.

They felt like something you could explore.

Chapter 3 — The Golden Age of Technology

The year was 2000.

Ecuador was going through a huge economic crisis. Inflation was through the roof, and I was almost ten years old. I didn't really understand what was happening.

I remember trying to buy an ice cream that cost about 1,000 Sucres. That was already cheap, but the old man selling it told me it was no longer 1,000 Sucres.

It was 25,000.

I remember thinking, "What?"

I didn't understand it at all.

Later, my parents explained what was happening. Ecuador changed its currency to the American Dollar. Desperate times called for desperate measures.

The transition affected everything, including technology. Price tags, imports, availability, and just about everything else took time to settle down.

Eventually, things became more stable, and I started enjoying technology again.

And then came what I consider the golden age of technology.

The 2000s.

At least, it was a golden age for me.

Everything looked amazing. Technology was becoming portable, colorful, transparent, and sometimes even see-through. Computers, phones, gadgets, cases, accessories, everything seemed to have its own personality.

There was a small office across the park that I used to visit with my friends and neighbors. They would throw away boxes full of old technology.

Floppy disks.

CDs.

Hard drives.

RAM.

Drives.

All kinds of things.

My brother and one of his friends asked the people at the office if they could take the boxes they were throwing away.

They said yes, but with one condition: if something broke our computer, it wasn't their fault.

My brother basically said, "Yeah, yeah, sure."

And that was the beginning of my journey into IT.

Most of the stuff was broken, to be honest. But some of it wasn't.

It was basically a 50/50 gamble.

We got free RAM upgrades, hard drives, floppy drives, CD-ROM drives, and all kinds of other hardware.

Why were they throwing all of it away?

The hard drives were often full of malware. The floppy and CD-ROM drives were frequently stuck.

I remember seeing my brother and his friend trying to open the drives and remove dirt from them. Sometimes we would even find toothpicks inside.

And then we discovered something that felt like magic.

There was a tiny hole on the front of the CD-ROM drive.

You could put a paper clip into it and manually eject the tray.

"Wait, what?"

That was another little piece of technology that suddenly made sense.

My brother and his friend also removed viruses from the used hard drives. That's where I learned how antivirus software worked.

At the time, Symantec Norton and Panda Security were among the antivirus products we could actually get here.

Then we found something even better.

Windows XP upgrade CDs.

Sealed.

New.

There were around ten of them.

We asked the office if they really didn't need them.

They said, "No, our computers are fine."

So we upgraded our Windows 98 computers to Windows XP for free.

There was also a Windows XP Home CD.

That's where I learned how to install Windows and format hard drives.

Back then, partitioning a hard drive wasn't something you just clicked through in a friendly graphical interface. You had to calculate sizes, type things in, and if you wanted partitions, you needed a calculator and some patience.

I loved it.

After that, I started building computers from the used parts that were being thrown away.

Or, at the very least, I tested every single component.

If something was broken, I tagged it as broken with one of those little orange sticker labels.

My brother and I turned our computer into a Frankenstein's monster.

RAM slots?

Full.

Hard drives?

Four of them.

IDE cables?

Master and slave.

Floppy drives?

Of course.

CD-ROM drives?

Absolutely.

It wasn't a powerful computer. It was still just a home PC.

But we had transformed our basic home PC into what I would call a "basic home PC deluxe."

And it was awesome.

Chapter 4 — Becoming "The Computer Guy"

Around this time, my mom bought me a Nokia 3100 as my first phone.

I was around 14 or 15.

It was a basic Nokia phone, but later my friend Nelson gave me a cable that could connect the phone to a computer and transfer photos, videos, and other files.

That felt incredible.

I started learning about Symbian and Series 40, and something clicked in my head.

Every device has an operating system.

Computers weren't the only things running software.

Phones had operating systems too.

At around the same time, home internet access was becoming more affordable. Internet access had already existed here for years, but it took a long time before regular homes could actually afford it.

Cyber cafés were everywhere.

We just called them "cybers."

For about one dollar, you could use a computer for an hour.

There was one cyber very close to my house, and that was where I learned a lot about networking.

I would go there with friends to play Counter-Strike, Warcraft 3, Call of Duty, and other games.

Those games also taught me something else.

You could modify them.

You could add custom maps, mods, files, and other things.

Sometimes downloading something directly over the internet didn't work, so I created folders like "custom-maps" or "mods" and shared them across the LAN.

My friends could open the shared folder from their computers and simply drag the files where they needed to go.

Basic stuff.

But it was networking.

And I was learning it without even realizing that I was learning it.

This was also when I started to "work."

I put work in quotation marks because I was basically just "the guy who knows about computers."

Maybe once or twice a week, I would help someone with their computer so I could make enough money to go to the movies with my friends or attend LAN parties on the weekends.

It was some of the most fun I had.

Some jobs were simply removing malware.

Some were reinstalling Windows.

Sometimes Windows wouldn't recognize an additional hard drive, so I would format it.

Sometimes I replaced hardware.

Sometimes I built computers.

It was basically everything I already knew how to do.

To me, it felt like solving a puzzle.

Or playing with LEGO.

Chapter 5 — Learning Linux

My mom eventually bought us another computer.

This time it came with Windows Vista and internet access.

It was a good computer.

And I loved Windows Vista.

I know a lot of people hated Aero Glass, but I absolutely loved it.

The transparency.

The glass effects.

The animations.

It looked incredible to me.

Windows XP had mostly been about changing the theme to something like the Zune Theme and leaving it there.

Vista felt different.

There were new places to explore, new settings, new things to customize.

At this point, having a good computer and internet access changed everything.

The fun times started becoming learning times.

And the best part was that I was learning while still playing games with my friends online.

Unfortunately, the cyber cafés were slowly dying.

Home internet had become affordable enough that people didn't need to go to them as often.

That made me sad.

Then, around 2009 or 2010, I went to a presentation about "open software" at a college.

They gave us an Ubuntu DVD.

I went straight home, created a partition just in case, and installed Ubuntu.

It was surprisingly easy.

For the first time, I noticed that software updates and packages were coming from a domain ending in .ec.

It was from my own country.

That was fascinating.

It made me wonder what else I could do with Ubuntu.

That's when I started learning about Linux.

I learned that Ubuntu was just a distribution, or "flavor," of Linux.

I really liked it.

The layout.

The icons.

The sounds.

The cursors.

Everything felt soft and calm.

But when I wanted to play games, I simply booted back into my Windows Vista partition.

Linux wasn't ready for all the games I wanted to play.

Wine was already pretty good, and some games had native Linux support, but not everything worked.

Eventually, I switched back to Windows after getting a copy of Windows 7.

And for a while, I forgot about Linux.

Chapter 6 — Game Servers and Networking

Windows 7 became my favorite version of Windows.

This was also when I started learning about almost everything related to technology.

My friends and I wanted to play games online, but there was a problem.

The routing between Ecuador and the United States was terrible.

We were playing with around 200 ms of latency.

So I started looking for a solution.

I learned that some games came with dedicated server tools through Steam.

That opened an entirely new world.

I learned how to create dedicated servers.

I learned about map rotations.

Configuration files.

Round timers.

Bomb timers in Counter-Strike.

How many rounds we were going to play.

Server settings.

Everything.

But then came the obvious question.

How were my friends going to connect to the server?

VPNs.

God bless VPNs.

I was fascinated by anything virtual, and VPNs were no exception.

We started using Hamachi from LogMeIn.

It worked like a charm.

Suddenly, we were effectively playing a LAN game over the internet with around 20 ms instead of 200 ms.

Every time we wanted to play another game, I started learning about how its dedicated server worked.

I would download the SDK if the game needed one.

Read the Wiki.

Find the configuration files.

Figure out where they went.

Figure out what I needed to create.

Did the game create the folders automatically the first time it started?

Or did I need to create them myself?

I didn't know.

So I searched.

And learned.

This was also when I really became "the computer guy."

Friends' parents started calling me.

Friends of friends started calling me.

People at work.

People at home.

Someone had a computer problem, and somehow they knew that I could probably fix it.

I just knew stuff.

And if I didn't know something, how hard could it be?

Did it have a Wiki?

I could read.

Was there a workaround?

I could search for it.

Drivers?

Easy.

Maintenance?

Just a lot of dust.

Building a computer from scratch?

I had been doing that since I was a kid.

It was like playing with LEGO, just more expensive.

I didn't realize that this was becoming a real skill.

I was just interested in computers.

I wanted to know why things broke.

How they broke.

Whether something was actually broken.

Or whether the computer was just being a computer.

And yes, I still hate wireless printers.

Chapter 7 — Android, Rooting and Breaking Things

I skipped Windows 8.

I don't think it was a very good operating system.

The tiles just weren't for me.

I kept learning through Windows 7, and every time I fixed something, I learned something new.

One printer in particular gave me a massive headache.

It kept trying to connect to the internet and automatically update its software.

There was no obvious way to disable it, and the software was required.

So I thought:

Fine.

I'll block its internet access.

That's how I learned more about firewalls, TCP, UDP, and the hosts file.

I hated that printer.

But it taught me a lot.

I also never completely abandoned Linux.

I started learning about virtual machines.

A computer inside your computer.

Download an ISO.

Create a virtual machine.

Choose how much CPU, RAM, and storage you want.

Install the operating system.

And suddenly you had another computer running inside your computer.

I loved the idea.

Around this time, my mom got me a Samsung S5 for my birthday.

It was a really good phone.

And once again, I started breaking things.

I learned about Odin, the software used to flash firmware onto Samsung devices.

I always wanted the latest version of Android, even when that version wasn't officially available in my country.

So I would flash firmware from another country.

Everything worked.

My SIM.

Wi-Fi.

Bluetooth.

Everything.

And then I learned something else.

Android uses the Linux kernel.

Linux again.

Modified operating systems were amazing.

I unlocked my bootloader.

Installed custom bootloaders.

Installed TWRP.

And then I discovered that TWRP could flash ZIP files.

You could do almost anything with it.

Eventually, I wanted access to system files.

That led me to rooting Android.

Once I had root access, I could access practically everything on the phone.

I could delete things that the manufacturer never intended me to delete.

I could completely destroy the system if I wanted to.

But instead, I started debloating.

The phone came with a lot of things I never used.

So I removed them.

I stripped down Samsung's TouchWiz.

I made it lighter.

Faster.

Snappier.

And, of course, I broke it.

Then I fixed it.

Then I broke it again.

Then I fixed it again.

Eventually, I had something that worked exactly the way I wanted.

And that led to another question.

Could I do the same thing with Windows?

Chapter 8 — Servers, Minecraft and the CLI

I started experimenting with Windows performance.

I wanted faster boot times.

A faster login.

A more responsive system.

I had used TuneUp Utilities, but it mostly did basic things like cleaning caches and removing unwanted files.

I wanted more.

I wanted Windows to feel as fast and clean as my modified Android phone.

So I started researching what Windows actually needed.

What each service did.

What each component was for.

Whether something was necessary.

What would happen if I removed it.

And if removing something broke Windows?

I'd fix it.

There were plenty of modified Windows 7 versions available online, but I didn't like them.

Modified hosts files?

No.

Modified firewall settings?

No.

Modified registry settings?

Not unless I knew exactly what they were doing.

I wanted my own version.

A clean Windows installation with only the things I actually needed.

I removed programs I didn't use.

Internet Explorer.

Windows Media Player.

Other components I had alternatives for.

I used Firefox instead of Internet Explorer.

VLC instead of Windows Media Player.

Eventually, I had my own lightweight Windows 7 setup.

It wasn't some ultimate version of Windows.

It was simply Windows configured the way I wanted it.

Then one day I logged into my computer and saw a notification:

"Your PC is ready for your free upgrade."

Windows 10.

I upgraded almost immediately.

People were complaining about it, and I didn't particularly love it at first either.

It looked different.

The corners were sharp.

Everything was rectangles and squares.

There was no Aero Glass.

There were some transparency effects and animations, but the overall design was completely different.

Microsoft had started using the word "apps" more often too, instead of simply calling everything programs.

It was strange at first.

But eventually, I got used to it.

I continued modifying Windows.

At the same time, I was using virtual machines to experiment with Linux.

I tried Linux Mint.

Fedora.

Arch Linux.

I learned about different package managers.

apt.

dnf.

pacman.

I liked Fedora.

I loved Arch.

Installing Arch from scratch was an experience in itself.

It was basically everything I liked about operating systems.

You build it yourself.

You install exactly what you need.

Nothing more.

Linux Mint became my comfortable choice, but Arch was the one that really matched how I liked to configure things.

Around this time, I also kept working with technology.

Fixing computers.

Building computers.

Solving problems.

Working with networking equipment.

Switches.

Routers.

The problem was always the ISPs.

Internet providers here often restrict access to router settings, which makes networking work much harder than it needs to be.

Need port forwarding?

Call the ISP.

Need to change something with NAT?

Call the ISP.

Need UPnP?

Call the ISP.

You basically have to convince them to let you configure the connection you are paying for.

But that's another story.

Then my friends and I started playing Minecraft.

We used the same VPN trick.

Virtual LAN.

It worked.

But sometimes we wanted a real dedicated server.

Sometimes I would leave my computer running overnight, but the game could eventually flag me as AFK or disconnect me to save resources.

So I started searching for dedicated Minecraft servers.

I found a forum where someone mentioned that you could run your own Minecraft server on a very old PC.

Headless.

No monitor.

No GUI.

Just a server.

That sounded perfect.

I took one of my old computers, an i5-4440 with 24 GB of DDR3 1333 MHz RAM and a 2 TB hard drive.

I downloaded a Debian ISO.

Installed Debian.

No graphical interface.

Just SSH.

I put the computer under my desk.

And then I started installing everything I needed for Minecraft.

I could have used tools that made the whole process easier, but I wanted to understand what was actually happening.

So I went through the configuration files.

I configured the server.

I learned how to run it.

I learned how my friends could connect.

And I did it all from the command line.

That was my first real taste of running a server for the sake of running a server.

Chapter 9 — Trying Programming

At one point, I was going to work on a project with a friend's dad.

The plan was to build around 20 computers for CAD work and set up a small server where everyone could store and download their projects.

We reviewed the budget.

I talked with my friend about the parts.

He was going to get everything.

And then COVID happened.

Everything stopped.

We were locked inside our homes.

I even got COVID twice.

The first time was horrible.

The second time wasn't nearly as bad.

During that period, I tried to learn programming.

It never really clicked with me.

I learned the basics of HTML and CSS for building websites.

I played around with JavaScript.

I looked into Ruby on Rails.

But eventually I realized that programming wasn't really where my interests were.

I could understand it.

I could make basic things.

But it wasn't what made me excited about technology.

So I went back to what I already knew.

Systems.

Computers.

Troubleshooting.

I continued earning money mostly by fixing computers remotely.

Removing viruses.

Installing drivers.

Troubleshooting.

Debloating Windows.

Improving performance.

Solving whatever problem someone had.

It wasn't a formal IT career.

But it was still IT work.

And I kept learning.

Chapter 10 — Finding My Way Back to Systems

Eventually, Windows 11 arrived.

I'm still using it today.

The initial transition had a few hiccups, but nothing major.

I started with a fresh installation.

And, once again, I found myself staring at a Windows installation filled with things I didn't want.

Preinstalled software.

Background applications.

AI features.

Web searches integrated into the operating system.

Things I didn't ask for.

So I did what I always do.

I debloated it.

I broke it.

I fixed it.

I repeated the process until I was happy.

Eventually, I had a Windows 11 installation with no unnecessary background applications, no AI features I didn't want, no web searching from the Start menu or taskbar, and basically just the computer.

I still use Windows as my daily driver.

But Linux never disappeared from my life.

I currently have Fedora KDE installed on my laptop.

It's a basic laptop.

It's a little old.

But it works.

And that's one of the things I love about Linux.

You don't necessarily need the newest hardware.

You can take an old computer, install the right operating system, configure it the way you want, and it can still be useful.

That philosophy has stayed with me for years.

Chapter 11 — Proxmox

Then, one day, I was cleaning up some old stuff that had been sitting around collecting dust.

And I found the old PC that had once been my Minecraft server.

The same machine.

The i5-4440.

The 24 GB of RAM.

The 2 TB hard drive.

I cleaned it.

Removed all the dust.

Applied fresh thermal paste.

Booted it up.

It worked.

And instead of selling it or throwing it away, I started thinking about what else I could do with it.

Then it hit me.

Proxmox.

I love Proxmox.

It was one of the things that really pulled me deeper into virtualization.

Proxmox VE is a hypervisor based on Debian.

Of course it is based on Debian.

All roads lead to Debian.

I installed it.

Started playing with it.

And then started thinking about projects.

The old Minecraft server didn't have to disappear.

I could put it inside Proxmox.

So I did.

I moved it into a Linux Container.

Now that old Minecraft server lives inside my homelab.

Maybe forever.

Or at least until that 2 TB hard drive finally gives up.

And that's when the idea of a real homelab started becoming much more interesting.

Snapshots.

Templates.

Virtual machines.

Containers.

Networking.

Storage.

Backups.

There were suddenly so many things I could learn.

The old computer wasn't just an old computer anymore.

It was a lab.

Chapter 12 — Docker and the Homelab

After Proxmox, I started looking into Docker.

Docker immediately made sense to me.

Technically, you run applications inside isolated environments, and the best part is that if it works inside the container, it should work the same way somewhere else.

That is exactly the kind of thing I like.

"It just works."

So I started building more things inside my Proxmox environment.

I currently have four VMs.

The first one is my Docker VM.

It runs Docker, Portainer, and Uptime Kuma.

The second one is my DNS server.

It runs AdGuard Home, which I use for local DNS and blocking unwanted ads.

The third one is my monitoring VM.

It runs Prometheus and Grafana.

And the fourth one is my Minecraft server.

What started as an old computer under my desk became a small infrastructure environment.

I'm learning virtualization.

Linux.

Docker.

Networking.

DNS.

Monitoring.

Containers.

Services.

And, more importantly, I'm learning how all of these things fit together.

This is probably the closest I've ever been to having the kind of environment I used to read about.

Not because it's huge.

It's not.

It's just a few virtual machines running on an old computer.

But it's mine.

And I built it myself.

And that brings me to today.

September 2026.

I'm 35 years old.

I've been around computers for most of my life.

From typing random words into my dad's old computer...

To playing with Windows 98...

To taking discarded hardware from an office...

To turning a basic home PC into a Frankenstein's monster...

To learning about networking through LAN games...

To becoming the person everyone called when their computer broke...

To learning Linux...

To rooting phones...

To breaking Windows and fixing it...

To running game servers...

To learning the command line...

To running a headless Debian server...

To discovering virtualization...

And finally, to building my own homelab with Proxmox, Docker, DNS, monitoring, and containers.

Looking back, it feels like a lot.

But at the same time, it doesn't.

Because I never really had a moment where I decided, "I'm going to learn IT."

That's probably the most important thing technology has taught me.

You don't need to know everything.

You need to know how to learn.

Chapter 14 — AI and the Future

I haven't talked much about AI throughout this story, but it deserves its own chapter.

I've watched AI evolve over the years.

I remember when generating weird images and strange videos felt like a novelty.

Now we're at a point where AI can generate software, images, videos, music, games, and all kinds of things that would have seemed impossible not that long ago.

I think AI is an incredible tool.

What I don't like is when people use it for absolutely everything.

When people stop thinking for themselves and simply say, "I'll let AI choose for me," it feels strange.

I don't want that.

I want AI to help me save time.

I want to understand what it gives me.

I want to learn from it.

And, yes, I'm literally using AI to turn this giant wall of text into a proper HTML page with CSS so I can put it on GitHub.

It saves me a lot of typing.

And honestly, that's exactly how I think technology should work.

Use the tool.

Understand the tool.

Let it save you time.

But don't let it replace your curiosity.

Because curiosity is what got me here in the first place.

I never planned this journey.

I just kept asking questions.

What is this?

How does it work?

Can I fix it?

Can I make it faster?

Can I modify it?

What happens if I change this?

What happens if I break it?

And if I break it...

Can I fix it?

That's been my journey through technology.

From that first computer I saw my dad using, to the homelab sitting in my home today.

And I'm not done yet.

It's September 2026.

I'm 35 years old.

And I will always love computers and technology.
